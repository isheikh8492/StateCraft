# World systems (draft v0.1)

Six systems the other tabs lean on, each designed to reuse the rules already written: pressure and thresholds, personas, mandates, the dice and the ledger.

## Events of every size

The world produces events all the time, from the absurd to the catastrophic. Only the largest are rationed.

| Size | Examples | How often | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Colour | A minister's gaffe, an affair, a leaked voice note, a viral joke, a feud over protocol, a bizarre local story | Constantly | None on numbers by itself; it gives the world texture and gives people something to talk about |
| Minor | A by-election, a strike, a cabinet spat, a court date, a diplomatic snub | Most days somewhere | Small, local, short-lived |
| Significant | A scandal with evidence, a major protest, a bank failure, a border clash, a resignation | Monthly or so in a busy country | Moves approval, markets or relations |
| Major | A coup, a war, a default, an assassination, a revolution | Rare, at historical rates | Changes the game |

- **Small things can grow.** An event can climb a size when conditions feed it. An affair becomes a scandal if a rival holds proof and the press is free. A gaffe becomes a resignation if the minister was already weak. A stupid controversy becomes a riot if grievance was already high. Growth follows the pressure rule and the dice.
- **Most things fizzle.** That is the base rate, and it is what makes the ones that grow feel earned.
- **Tone varies.** The game can be funny. Politics produces farce as often as tragedy, and colour events are written in whatever register fits.
- **They involve you too.** Your own gaffes, your family, your old statements and your subordinates' blunders are all material.

**Real people are limited to public conduct.** Affairs, family matters, health and other private-life events are generated only for fictional characters. For a real, living person the game generates events about public acts only: statements, votes, decisions, appointments and appearances.

## Intelligence services

Everything you know beyond what is public comes from your intelligence services, and they are institutions with limited reach, their own interests and a chief with a persona. Your picture of the world is theirs.

- **Capabilities.** Each service is rated on human sources, intercepts, analysis, covert action and counter-intelligence, per region of the world. A service strong at home can be blind abroad.
- **You set priorities.** Collection is limited. You choose a handful of priority targets, and everything else gets thin coverage. What you did not ask about is where surprises come from.
- **The daily brief.** Reports arrive with a confidence level and a source type. Some carry a dissent note where analysts disagree.
- **Reports can be wrong** in three ways: the service could not see it, the other side deceived it, or the service told you what you wanted to hear. The third grows as fear of you grows.
- **Sources are people.** An agent inside a rival's cabinet is a character who can be caught, turned or lost. Acting on what they tell you risks exposing them.
- **Counter-intelligence.** Other services are doing the same to you. Moles, leaks and bugged rooms are found only if you look, and looking sows suspicion among your own people.
- **The service has its own agenda.** It can hold things back, run operations you did not approve, and keep files on you. Its chief is among the most dangerous people you appoint.
- **Sharing.** Allies trade intelligence. What they give is shaped by what they want you to believe.
- **The map is your intelligence picture.** Foreign units and unrest appear where your services believe them to be, with uncertainty shown.

## Elections

Elections run on the country's real calendar and real electoral system, and you can lose them.

- **The system matters.** Seats are worked out the way that country does it: constituency by constituency, by party list, in two rounds, or through an electoral college. The same national vote gives different parliaments under different systems.
- **Support comes from the approval groups.** Each party's vote in each region is built from how the groups living there feel about it, plus local candidates and turnout.
- **The campaign is a period of play** at day-by-day tempo. What you can do during it:
  - make manifesto promises, which the ledger tracks afterwards as commitments
  - form alliances and share seats
  - hold rallies and debates and work the media
  - raise and spend money
  - time your policies
  - use dirty tricks, at a risk of exposure
- **Polls are estimates.** They carry error, and some pollsters lean.
- **Election night plays on the map,** region by region, as results come in.
- **Then comes the bargaining.** In systems without a clear winner, forming a government is a negotiation over ministries and policy, held in conversation.
- **Results can be disputed.** Recounts, court challenges, street protest and refusal to concede follow the usual rules of pressure and institutions.
- **Rigging is possible** where institutions are weak enough, with its own risks (see the authoritarian path).
- **Other countries vote too.** Their elections are simulated at lower detail and can replace the leaders you were dealing with.

## Media and information

People react to what they hear happened, not to what happened. Between every event and every opinion sits the media, and the fight over the story is a game of its own.

- **Outlets are actors.** Each newspaper, channel and big online account has an owner, a leaning, a reach into specific approval groups, and a credibility score.
- **Every event gets competing versions.** When something happens, outlets and politicians offer rival framings ("reform" or "betrayal", "security operation" or "massacre"). Each group ends up believing the version carried by the outlets it follows and trusts.
- **Approval moves on the believed version.** The engine keeps the true event and each group's perceived event side by side. A success nobody hears about earns nothing, and a failure that is buried costs nothing until it surfaces.
- **Your tools:** press conferences, interviews, favoured leaks, friendly owners, advertising money, your own party media, timing bad news, and at the hard end censorship, shutdowns and propaganda.
- **Credibility is a stock.** Every lie that is caught drains yours. A government nobody believes cannot calm a panic even when it tells the truth.
- **Censorship has a price.** It hides events from some groups and tells everyone something is being hidden. Rumour fills the gap.
- **Foreign influence.** Other states fund outlets and run online campaigns in your country, and you can do it in theirs.
- **Social media moves fastest.** A clip or a hashtag can make a colour event significant within hours.

## Trade, money and sanctions

Countries are tied together by what they buy, sell, lend and owe, and those ties are both a lifeline and a lever.

- **Trade by sector.** Flows between countries are tracked in a few sectors: energy, food, raw materials, manufactured goods, technology, arms and remittances. The pipeline builds them from real trade data.
- **Dependence is the key number.** For each pair, the engine knows how much each side needs the other and how quickly it could find a substitute. That decides who has leverage.
- **Sanctions are objects** with a sender coalition, a scope (people, sectors, banks, everything), enforcement strength and leakage. Targets route around them through third countries, at a cost. Senders pay too, in lost trade and in pressure from their own businesses.
- **Chokepoints.** Straits, pipelines, ports and cables are places on the map. Closing or threatening one affects every flow that passes through.
- **Money.** Currency, reserves and borrowing costs follow the budget rules. Creditors are actors: the IMF, development banks and lender states each attach conditions and can roll over or call in debt.
- **Companies are actors.** Large firms and tycoons lobby, invest, threaten to leave, fund parties and dodge sanctions.
- **Deals are commitments.** Trade agreements, loans and investment pledges are recorded in the ledger with terms and deadlines, and breaking them has a price.

## International bodies

International organisations work like governments in miniature: members, procedures, votes and officials, with the same pressure and persuasion as everything else.

| Body | What it does in the game |
| --- | --- |
| UN Security Council | Resolutions that authorise force or sanctions; five permanent members hold vetoes |
| UN General Assembly | Votes that confer or deny legitimacy; a count of who stands with you |
| IMF and development banks | Loans with conditions that reach into your budget |
| Alliances | Mutual defence promises whose credibility is tested in a crisis |
| Regional blocs | Trade rules, summits, peer pressure, and a stage for regional rivalries |
| International courts | Rulings and warrants that are slow, hard to enforce and costly to ignore |
| Informal clubs | Groupings of major economies or like-minded states where deals are made before the formal vote |

- **Votes are won in conversation.** Before a vote you lobby members one by one, trade favours and count heads. Each member decides by its own interests and its relations with you.
- **Procedures are real.** Vetoes, majorities, rotating chairs and agenda rules come from each body's actual rules.
- **Legitimacy is the currency.** A resolution rarely forces anyone, but it changes what others will tolerate, join or punish.
- **Summits are events** on the calendar, where many leaders are reachable in one place for side meetings.
- **Membership can change.** States join, leave, are suspended or form rival bodies.
